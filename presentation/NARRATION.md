## Slide 1: Shadow Masque  [0:00 to 0:15 (15 s)]

This is Shadow Masque, a competitive two-player stealth platformer built on a reconstruction of Prince of Persia's first level. Two rival swordsmen share one palace. Each can disguise himself as a guard, set traps in the floor, and try to eliminate the other. What I want a player to feel is tension and uncertainty: is that figure a guard, or my rival? I'm Saad Satter, and this is the G2/G3 first playable.

## Slide 2: First Playable: live demo  [0:15 to 1:15 (60 s)]

Here is the loop I will show live: I act, the game responds, I interpret what I see, and choose my next action. I'll start the game in AI Model mode. I move through the palace and drop into the Barracks, where a scripted guard patrols. I draw my sword, strike the guard down, and take its uniform. Now I'm disguised, and the guard's suspicion rises if I behave oddly, for example if I hop. Then I go looking for the rival. This rival is the trained PPO policy, and it only sees what my character's side of the game shows it. When it appears, I decide whether to fight, flee or use a loose-floor trap, and what it does next changes what I do. Note: I'm playing this live, so what the rival does is whatever the policy decides in that moment; the screenshots are a scripted rehearsal and I will say so if I use them.

## Slide 3: The AI rival: learned vs scripted  [1:15 to 1:55 (40 s)]

The rival is a small PPO policy that I trained, and it sits on the same interface as a scripted baseline. It sees only its own seat's partial observation, chooses a macro action from the legal list, and the one game engine runs it. On the held-out evaluation, 16 duels, the learned rival won 13, drew 2 and lost 1 against the scripted baseline. The bar we registered in advance was 14 wins, so the verdict is not supported. That means the improvement was not shown at this bar, not that the rival is weak. Also, the three trials of each duel were identical, so the effective sample is closer to eight starts. What I observed is that strength. What I have not observed is what players experience: that it is harder to read when disguised, or more tense, are hypotheses I have not tested. Cost: training ran locally on CPU in about 80 minutes, with no API spend, and no paid model call has been made in the final project.

## Slide 4: External playtest: Human vs AI  [1:55 to 2:40 (45 s)]

This is the one external playtest so far: a player outside the team played against the trained rival in a hosted duel, with the host logging telemetry. I've reconstructed the clip from the logged inputs. It is a replay, not a recording: the game engine is deterministic, so replaying the logged inputs gives the same duel, and I checked the final state against the log's hash. The label says so. Here are my observer notes, in my words from the playtest log: the player could identify the rival and the rival could locate the player, strong combat, effective against guards, and the player struggled to counter it. Beside them, what the replay itself shows: three hits on the human, at frames 417, 441 and 465; the AI won with two hearts left; the only dropped sword is the human's own throw in the last moments; and a guard goes down at frame 327 with no attacker recorded. So the replay supports the strong-combat impression, and not every item in my notes. My interpretation was that combat might need balancing, but this is one player and one duel. The open question: can players develop counterstrategies against this rival, or does combat need balancing?

## Slide 5: Production agent team  [2:40 to 3:20 (40 s)]

Production runs on a clear chain. I'm the Student, Executive Producer and Creative Director. An Opus orchestrator routes work to Sonnet agents. Two of them are the formal course agents: the Tech Lead, who implements the engine, agent interface, builds and technical evidence, and QA, who verifies independently, reproduces failures and checks the observation boundary. The Evaluation Specialist, Requirements Auditor and rules reviewer are supporting workers, and the in-game PPO rival is a game feature, not a production agent. The real handoff is T02. QA found that a disguised player who waited could be told apart from a paused guard, because of unintended motion metadata. That would break the core stealth idea. I approved the fix, including hopping raising suspicion, six per frame, forty-eight per hop, and treating DOWN as standing still. The Tech Lead fixed it, QA independently verified it with 650 tests passing, 12 skipped and 2 unrelated expected failures, and I accepted it.
